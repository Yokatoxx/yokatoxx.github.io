Project summary

ShadowLight is a project created during the 48-hour Winter GameJam around the theme “Two in One”, in collaboration with Noah Caux.

As Lead Programmer, I worked on turning design intentions into playable systems, with a focus on dimension switching, enemy vulnerability and replayability.

Design challenge

The theme required us to create a mechanic that genuinely expressed the idea of “two in one” while remaining achievable within the limited time of a game jam.

We considered several directions, including split-screen co-op and controlling two characters. We ultimately chose a dimension-switching mechanic, which was more directly connected to the theme and more realistic to prototype quickly.

Expressing the theme through a mechanic

The world is inhabited by ghosts from two dimensions. The player can only attack a ghost while standing in that ghost’s home dimension.

I started by implementing the state change and vulnerability logic. This let us validate the core feel before adding upgrades or secondary systems.

Building a replayable loop

Once the combat loop worked, we identified replayability as its main weakness. We chose to add a soul economy instead of producing more levels or enemies.

This roguelite-like choice created meaningful decisions without requiring new assets or significant production time. It also fit naturally into the die-and-retry loop.

Managing scope

During a 48-hour game jam, every feature had to be assessed by its cost and benefit. We cut or postponed ideas that did not improve the experience enough to justify the required time.

Question used during production
Does this feature improve the experience enough to justify the time it costs?

What I learned

This jam strengthened my instinct for prioritization. A focused, properly executed mechanic is more valuable than an ambitious idea left unfinished.

Technically, dimension switching required careful state management to avoid audio and visual issues during transitions. It pushed me to write cleaner, more modular code.

The project also taught me to connect a theme constraint more directly to a concrete gameplay loop, then protect that loop from feature creep.
